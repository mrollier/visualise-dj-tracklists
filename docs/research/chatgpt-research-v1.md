# 1. Taxonomy of approaches  
**Taxonomy-based measures.** Traditional semantic-similarity metrics from natural-language taxonomies (like WordNet) can be applied to a genre hierarchy.  Simple **edge-count** or **shortest‐path** distance (possibly normalized by depth) treats parent-child edges uniformly.  Variants include Leacock–Chodorow (depth-normalized log-distance) and Wu–Palmer (depth of lowest common ancestor).  **Information-content (IC) measures** (Resnik, Lin, Jiang–Conrath) use corpus statistics to weight concept specificity.  These assume a well-curated hierarchy of genres.  In practice, music genre ontologies are incomplete or inconsistent – e.g. Wikidata’s “genre” graph has only a single “subclass” relation and is very sparse.  Aucouturier & Pachet note that genre itself is *“ill-defined, not founded on any intrinsic property of the music, but rather depends on cultural… habits”*.  Thus taxonomy-based similarity depends entirely on the chosen hierarchy’s quality.  **Strengths:** clear interpretation, exploits known parent/subgenre structure, very cheap to compute once a tree is built.  **Weaknesses:** fails if the hierarchy is shallow or mismatches real usage, cannot handle polyhierarchies or dangling genres well, and doesn’t capture genre mixing.  Multi-label taxa can be partially handled (e.g. a track in “House” and “Techno” would connect both subtrees), but thresholding is tricky.  In summary, classic semantic measures (path length, Wu–Palmer, Leacock–Chodorow) yield symmetric [0,1] scores naturally (often by normalizing with a global max distance). IC measures (Resnik, Lin) require a reference corpus for probability estimates.  

**Distributional/co-occurrence methods.** These derive genre similarity from how genre labels co-occur in data such as user tags, artist discographies, or playlists.  One can form a genre-by-context co-occurrence matrix (contexts = tracks, users, albums, etc.) and compute **(Positive) Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI/PPMI)** to weight each genre’s context vector.  Dimensionality-reduction (SVD, NMF) or random projection then yields dense embeddings for each genre.  Alternatively, apply neural embeddings (Word2Vec/Glove) by treating tracks or playlists as “sentences” of genre tokens.  Graph embeddings (e.g. node2vec) can learn vector representations from a genre-tag graph.  These methods assume ample data of real-world genre labels (tag/taxonomy *folksonomy*).  **Strengths:** Capture empirical semantic closeness, adapt to multi-genre usage, can uncover non-hierarchical relations (e.g. “House” ~ “Techno”).  **Weaknesses:** Require large genre-label corpora (tags or playlists); noisy free-text labels can distort co-occurrence counts (e.g. synonyms, multi-label strings).  They produce continuous cosine-similarities (symmetric by construction) in [0,1] after normalization.  They gracefully handle unseen labels only if the label’s tokens appear in training (out-of-vocab means no embedding).  The MediaEval AcousticBrainz task and related work use PPMI+SVD embeddings on multi-source tag data.  Sordo/Celma’s LSA experiments showed that folksonomy-derived semantics significantly correlate with expert-taxonomy distances (genres at distance 1 had much higher tag-based cosine than distant pairs), validating this approach.  

**Audio-content methods.** These compute similarity from the raw music signal.  For each track, extract acoustic features (e.g. Essentia/MirFeatures, MFCCs, timbre) or pretrained **audio embeddings**.  Example embeddings include MusiCNN (200-D learned by a CNN), OpenL3 (512-D contrastive audio–image embedding), or CLAP (contrastive audio–text embedding).  Vectors for tracks can be averaged per genre or used to train a genre classifier.  **Strengths:** Reflect actual sound/music similarities; open models (e.g. CLAP, AudioSet/VGGish) enable client-side use.  **Weaknesses:** Genre boundaries are often cultural, so acoustically distinct tracks can share a genre or vice versa.  Audio methods require access to track signals or precomputed features (e.g. AcousticBrainz’s Essentia fingerprints).  Computational cost is high (feature extraction on-device is expensive), but a small set of precomputed genre-centroid vectors could be shipped.  The score (e.g. cosine distance of genre centroids) is symmetric.  Evidence of quality depends on the genre; some work shows audio features alone have limited power for high-level genre classification.  

**Hybrid methods.** One can combine the above.  For example, semantic and audio distances might be fused or ensembled.  Graph-based approaches (like MusicBrainz’s mo:Genre with an added *mo:similar_to* link) could combine label and signal info.  A hybrid might weight taxonomy distance with embedding cosine.  **Strengths:** potentially best of both worlds (semantic + perceptual).  **Weaknesses:** complexity of calibration, possibly asymmetric if combining, and larger data.  Example: Pachet’s (2000) *Taxonomy of Musical Genres* used hand-built parent/child plus extra “similar” links. In practice, hybrid systems are not well-codified in literature; most genre-sim work focuses on either tags or audio.  

**Computational and data costs:** Taxonomy methods need only a small graph (hundreds of nodes) – trivial to run. Distributional embeddings require storing co-occ matrices or embedding vectors; a 24–100D embedding per genre is cheap (few MB). Audio methods need either a precomputed feature/embedding bank or on-device model (often large). In all cases a symmetric [0,1] similarity can be returned (use cosine similarity or normalized path/IC). Table 1 (below) summarizes these methods.

# 2. Data sources for genre relations  
We survey available genre taxonomies and tag datasets, noting license and redistributability:

- **MusicBrainz (“style” tags)**: MB provides genre/style tags on releases, but *all* user-contributed annotations (including genres) are “supplementary” data licensed under CC BY-NC-SA.  Thus MB genre data cannot be redistributed in a commercial app.  (MB core data – e.g. artist names – is CC0, but genres are not core.)
- **Wikidata/DBpedia**:  Wikidata’s genre items (Q8789 etc.) are CC0 (public domain), but currently only the “instance of/subclass” (P279/P31) relations are used for musical genres, yielding a shallow tree.  DBpedia (from Wikipedia) has more detailed infobox links (often *stylistic origin* or category), but DBpedia data is CC BY-SA (free with attribution).  These ontologies could be used if carefully filtered, since CC BY-SA is allowed in an app with credit.
- **Discogs**:  Discogs release metadata (genres and styles per release/master) is available via monthly data dumps under CC0.  A useful taxonomy can be built from Discogs’s fixed set of genres/styles (CC0).  (Discogs free-text artist genres are generally just combined from track tags, but the genre/style list itself is controlled.)  Thus Discogs is fully redistributable.
- **Last.fm**:  The Last.fm tag hierarchy (e.g. LFM1b dataset) is copyrighted; the standard public dump is CC BY-NC-SA or research-only, so it cannot be freely shipped.  In practice one can mine Last.fm via API for personal use, but its data (especially real-time tag counts) is under restrictive terms.
- **Every Noise at Once / MusicMap**:  These interactive genre maps (by Glenn McDonald, Peter van der Kloet, etc.) compile exhaustive genre lists, but have no open license published.  They are essentially proprietary “visualizations” of Echo Nest/Pandora data.  The raw data or mapping cannot be redistributed.
- **Beatport (Genre Taxonomy)**:  Beatport’s genre/subgenre list is not publicly licensed; it’s proprietary to Beatport.  Cannot be redistributed.
- **FMA (Free Music Archive dataset)**:  FMA provides metadata for 106,574 CC-licensed audio tracks, including a hierarchical taxonomy of 161 genres (17 top-level genres plus sub-genres).  Critically, FMA’s *metadata* (including genre taxonomy) is CC BY 4.0.  This means its genre hierarchy can be freely used in a commercial app (with attribution).  The actual audio in FMA is CC-licensed per track, but we only need the taxonomy file (which FMA freely provides) for genre names and relations.
- **AcousticBrainz Genre Dataset**:  As used in MediaEval, this combines genre labels from AllMusic, Discogs, Last.fm, Tagtraum.  The *resulting metadata* is CC BY-NC-SA (non-commercial); in fact, AllMusic labels were only for research.  Therefore, the AcousticBrainz multi-source labels *cannot* be packaged for commercial use.  The *audio features* in AcousticBrainz are under free licenses (Essentia features CC0), but the *genre annotations* are not.
- **AudioSet**:  While not a genre database, Google’s AudioSet provides 10-second clips annotated with some music-class concepts (e.g. “Music”, “Pop music”).  AudioSet labels are CC BY 4.0.  It could be used for audio-based genre modeling, but its genre labels are few (“Hip Hop music”, etc.).  The dataset is CC BY, so offline use is permitted.
- **Other corpora**:  Wikipedia and DBpedia content (CC BY-SA) could be mined for genre relations (e.g. links on genre pages).  Crowdsourced tag datasets (Last.fm, Tagtraum, Discogs user tags) may exist, but most are non-commercial-research-only or CC BY-NC.  

**Summary:**  The only fully open, redistributable genre taxonomy is the FMA genre hierarchy (CC BY).  Wikidata (CC0) is open but sparse.  Discogs (CC0) offers a flat genre/style list.  AcousticBrainz (AllMusic) and Last.fm are off-limits.  In practice, shipping a genre-graph pack could rely on FMA (free) and possibly supplement with Discogs (free) or DBpedia (CC BY-SA).  

# 3. Key papers (and sources)  
- **Aucouturier & Pachet (2003)**: *“Representing Musical Genre: A State of the Art”* (Journal of New Music Research).  They argue that **genre is ill-defined**: any strict definition tends to be circular.  They survey many approaches and conclude genre is culturally constructed, not purely audio-based.  This seminal paper casts doubt on purely audio-based genre classification.  
- **Sordo & Celma et al. (ISMIR 2008)**: *“The Quest for Musical Genres”*.  They compare expert taxonomies (AllMusic) versus bottom-up folksonomies (Last.fm tags) using LSA.  They show a strong statistical correlation between taxonomy distance and tag-based cosine similarity: genres close in a hierarchy tend to co-occur in user tags.  This supports using distributional methods to recover genre relations.  
- **Schreiber (ISMIR 2015)**: *“Improving Genre Annotations for a Million Song Dataset”*.  He compiles ~1M free-text user genre labels and applies systematic **normalization**: lowercasing, punctuation removal, splitting on delimiters, synonyms (e.g. “DnB”→“Drum and Bass”), etc.  This reduces 700k raw labels to ~71k normalized terms.  He finds a very skewed distribution (top 131 terms cover 80% of songs).  These normalization rules are useful for any text-based genre matching.  
- **Schreiber (ISMIR 2016)**: *“Genre Ontology Learning: Comparing Curated with Crowdsourced”*.  He automatically builds a genre hierarchy from co-occurrence of tags in the Million Song Dataset, then compares it with DBpedia/Wikidata.  E.g. his rule “if Tag A co-occurs significantly more with B than vice versa, declare A a subgenre of B” is given explicitly.  He finds the learned ontology has hundreds of genres and rich links, whereas curated sources (DBpedia/Wikidata) are sparse.  This work illustrates both taxonomy-based and distributional approaches.  
- **Bogdanov et al. (ISMIR 2019)**: *“The AcousticBrainz Genre Dataset”*.  Describes a large multi-source, multi-label genre collection (2M songs annotated by AllMusic, Discogs, Last.fm, TagTraum).  It highlights that genre annotation is multi-label and highly hierarchical, with hundreds of subgenres in some sources.  (MediaEval 2017/18 papers by the same authors discuss difficulty of classifying these data.)  This dataset is a key resource on genre distributions, though its license is CC BY-NC-SA.  
- **Li & Ogihara (ICASSP 2005)**: *“Music Genre Classification with Taxonomy”*.  They show that incorporating a given genre hierarchy improves classifier accuracy, and even propose *learning* a taxonomy from confusion matrices.  They conclude that “using taxonomy improves classification performance”.  This underscores the value of hierarchical relations in genre work.  
- **Pachet (RIAO 2000)**: *“A Taxonomy of Musical Genres”*.  Although older, this work built a large hand-crafted genre taxonomy (378 genres, 800 “similarity” links) through an editorial process.  It shows one can manually encode expert knowledge; however, it was very labor-intensive.  
- **Sturm & Gouyon (IEEE Signal Processing Lett. 2013)**: *“Revisiting Inter-Genre Similarity”*.  While focused on classification, they revisit the notion of “inter-genre similarity” (IGS) – acknowledging that some styles blur together.  They modify classifiers by modeling cross-genre confusion.  Their work is less on genre distance per se, but it highlights that genres form a continuum, supporting fuzzy/continuous measures.  
- **Aucouturier & Pachet (ICMC 2004)**: *“Improving Audio-Based Similarity and Music Genre Classification”*.  They note that pure audio features often fail and genre definitions vary by community.  This early paper suggests the limitations of audio-only methods.  

Other practitioner sources include the Music Ontology specification (defines a `mo:Genre` class and a `similar_to` property), the essetsia library docs on MusiCNN embeddings, and Sonos’s *Every Noise at Once* (a visual genre map).  However, few additional peer-reviewed works specifically address genre *distance*.  

# 4. Semantics questions (from literature)  

- **Symmetry of genre similarity.**  Most formal similarity measures (cosine, path length, Wu–Palmer, etc.) are symmetric by construction.  However, genre relations can be inherently asymmetrical: e.g. “Techno” may be a subgenre of “Electronic”, so one might argue “Techno is contained in Electronic” but Electronic covers more.  Asymmetric measures (like KL divergence or set containment ratios) could capture this directionality.  The literature on semantic similarity (from NLP) usually treats similarity as symmetric (relatedness).  Given our UI’s single threshold, we should enforce symmetry (otherwise the UI logic breaks).  In practice one could ensure `similarity(A,B)=similarity(B,A)` (e.g. define sim(A,B) = cos(vA,vB)).  If using a hierarchy, one could use `max(sim(A,B), sim(B,A))` to handle child-vs-parent.  There is no consensus study specifically on asymmetric genre similarity, so we recommend symmetric measures for simplicity, accepting that parent vs child will have equal weight on either side.  

- **Multi-genre and umbrella labels.**  Multi-genre tags (e.g. “Melodic House & Techno”) and very broad labels (“Electronic”, “Rock”) pose problems: they act as hubs connecting many styles.  The literature suggests treating them carefully.  For text/embedding methods, a simple step is to **split multi-genre strings** on “&” or “/” and treat each component separately (e.g. “Melodic House” and “Techno”).  Broad labels like “Electronic” might receive a very generic vector.  A common remedy is to **downweight generic tags** (analogous to stop-words) by an inverse-frequency (tf-idf) or simply exclude them from matching thresholds.  In handcrafted taxonomies, one might mark “Electronic” as a parent of “House”, “Trance”, etc., but then disallow it from creating false positives between its children.  There is no single best method, but empirically it often helps to *exclude* purely umbrella genres when computing similarity (or require at least two matching subgenres).  

- **Global threshold vs per-genre.**  A single global similarity threshold is simplest, but different genres have different “neighborhood densities”.  The literature on nearest-neighbor or clustering often recommends a fixed *k* or rank-based approach rather than an absolute cutoff.  Some works (e.g. in tag recommendation) use top-*k* nearest neighbors per genre to ensure each genre has a consistent number of links.  However, user interface simplicity may favor one threshold.  One can evaluate by looking at the distribution of similarity scores: e.g. require that the threshold is above random-similarity levels.  In absence of conclusive guidance, a safe approach is: use the global threshold for simplicity, but consider allowing “must match all tokens” for lexical overlap to reduce spurious hubs, effectively making the lexical measure more like a rank.  Ultimately, this is an empirical tuning question.  

# 5. Evaluation methodology  
Evaluating a genre-similarity measure is challenging because “ground truth” is fuzzy.  Common approaches:  

- **Human judgments.**  Create a test set of genre-pairs (or triplets) and have human experts or crowdworkers rate their similarity.  For instance, one could randomly sample ~50–100 genre-label pairs covering easy and hard cases (e.g. “House–Techno”, “House–Classical”, “House–Deep House”).  Then obtain similarity ratings (e.g. 1–5 scale) or relative judgments (“which of these pairs is more similar?”) from multiple listeners.  Metrics: compute Spearman or Kendall rank correlation between algorithmic scores and average human scores.  Alternatively, binarize “similar” vs “not” and measure accuracy (precision/recall) against majority vote.  For reliability, aim for ≥3–5 ratings per pair; ~50 pairs often gives enough data for a meaningful Spearman correlation (p<0.05 if ρ ≈0.5).  A forced-choice “top-k” test is also possible: ask users to pick the *most similar* genre to a target out of a list of 5, and see if the algorithm’s top choice matches the majority user choice.  

- **Expert taxonomy agreement.**  Use an existing genre hierarchy (AllMusic or FMA) as a proxy “ground truth”.  For every pair of genres in the taxonomy, define a “gold” distance (e.g. path length or 1 for same parent).  Then compute the Spearman correlation between that distance and the algorithm’s score over many genre pairs.  This was done by Sordo et al. and by Schreiber as a sanity check.  It gives a quantitative measure of how well the measure recovers known structure.  

- **Downstream tasks.**  Indirect evaluation via related tasks (e.g. playlist continuity or recommendation): e.g. measure if using the similarity improves playlist generation or user satisfaction.  This is more complex and dataset-specific, but one could test whether thresholding by the measure yields better DJ set transitions (if such data exists).  

- **Proposed concrete test.**  We recommend a mix: assemble ~50 genre pairs (drawn from the FMA taxonomy and some common idioms) and ask, say, 5 knowledgeable DJs/enthusiasts to rate similarity (1–5).  Compute Spearman’s ρ between each of our four measures and the mean rating; also compute pairwise accuracy if thresholded at “>=3 = similar”.  For reliability, use at least 5 raters per pair.  A Spearman ρ≥0.5 or accuracy ≥80% would indicate a reasonably good measure given subjective variability.  (Prior work in NLP often considers ρ≥0.6 as acceptable on similarity datasets.)  This low-effort study could validate ranking order or threshold choices without extensive costs.  

# 6. Actionable recommendations and implementation steps  

**Recommended measures:**  Given the constraints (client-side, small data, CC-licensed, symmetric [0,1] score, EDM/electronic focus) and the four existing methods, we suggest the following tiered approach:

1. **Keep lexical matching as fallback.**  Continue using exact-match and token-Jaccard on normalized labels (as in methods 1–2) for obvious synonyms (e.g. “DnB” vs “Drum and Bass”).  This is trivial and covers very clear cases.

2. **Expand/replace curated taxonomy with FMA hierarchy.**  The hand-built graph (~90 genres) is useful but limited.  Instead, import the FMA genre hierarchy (161 CC-BY genres plus parents).  This supplies a broader structure with open license.  Build a mapping table of synonyms and alias (using Schreiber’s normalization rules).  Retain parent-child links and compute a path-based similarity: for two genres, compute the distance in the FMA tree (e.g. shortest edges) and convert to [0,1] (e.g. `sim = decay^distance`).  This similarity is symmetric and yields 1 for identical genre or direct synonyms.  Because FMA’s root genres overlap with electronic and global categories, it will help moderate broad matches (“Electronic” vs subgenres).  

3. **Use distributional embedding (PPMI + truncated SVD).**  Our current PPMI+random vector method can be improved.  Recommendation: use PPMI on an open corpus and perform a **truncated SVD** (not random projection).  Specifically, crawl or compile a genre co-occurrence matrix from *discogs* data (CC0): for each release in the Discogs dump (genres+styles fields), increment co-occurrence of its genre/style tags.  Compute the PPMI matrix of genre×genre, then apply SVD to reduce to ~24–50 dimensions.  Store each genre’s 24-D vector (floating values).  At runtime, similarity = cosine(vectorA, vectorB).  SVD ensures maximal retention of structure in 24 dims; random projection is fast but less stable/optimal.  With ~150 genres, a 24-D SVD is very lightweight (150×24 floats).  This embedding will capture the *folksonomy* semantics of how real DJs/artists label tracks.  Because Discogs is CC0 and FMA tags (if any) are CC-BY, the resulting vectors are redistributable.  

4. **Optionally incorporate audio embedding for refinement.**  If feasible, a very lightweight content signal could be added.  For example, use precomputed **AudioSet features** (128-D VGGish or CLAP embeddings) averaged per genre from a small reference set of prototypical tracks.  Then define similarity as a weighted sum: `w1*lex+ w2*tax+ w3*embed+ w4*audio`.  In practice, we suspect distributional+taxonomy will suffice for this app (since users tune thresholds anyway), but including an audio component (if already computed) could marginally help very acoustically distinctive cases.  

**Comparison to existing methods:**  The proposed FMA taxonomy replaces the manual graph (likely improving coverage) and remains symmetric by design.  Embedding (SVD) improves over PPMI+randproj by providing stable orthogonal dimensions.  Lexical and exact match are unchanged.  We suggest *keeping* all four original criteria (exact, lexical, curated, embedding) but with these enhancements: the curated graph now FMA-based, and embedding computed via SVD.  All yield a [0,1] cosim or path score.  The random-projection step can be dropped; SVD-computed vectors preserve more nuance (empirically, SVD is better for small vocabularies).  

**Data pack and format:**  Ship two small files: (a) **Taxonomy file** (JSON) listing each genre and its parent(s) from FMA (and any added synonyms), with edge weights (or implicit weight=1).  (b) **Embedding file** (JSON or binary) mapping each genre to a 24-D vector (float32).  Also include a synonym map (e.g. “Melodic House & Techno” → [“Melodic House”, “Techno”]).  Together these are a few megabytes at most.  All data (FMA genres and Discogs co-occurrence counts) are open-licensed.  At runtime, for a label pair (A,B): normalize labels, check exact/synonym match first; otherwise compute taxonomy-sim (via tree distance) and embedding-sim (cosine of vectors).  Finally take `sim = max(lex_sim, tax_sim, embed_sim)` (or a weighted combination; a simple max or average will do).  The result is symmetric and in [0,1].  

**Implementation steps:**  1. **Data preparation:** Download FMA metadata (CC BY) and Discogs dumps (CC0).  Extract FMA genre hierarchy (genres.csv) and normalize names.  Extract Discogs genres/styles for each release, build co-occurrence counts of all genre-label pairs.  2. **Compute embedding:** From the co-occurrence matrix, compute PPMI and perform a 24-D SVD (e.g. using numpy/scipy).  3. **Build taxonomy graph:** Use FMA’s hierarchy plus any synonyms as a tree/forest; optionally prune or re-root if needed.  4. **Store data pack:** Serialize the graph (parents list) and embedding vectors into JSON or a compact binary (e.g. Float32Array).  5. **Similarity function:** In the app, implement normalization (lowercase, remove punctuation, split on “&”, etc.).  Then for each genre pair: (a) check if identical or in alias map → return 1; (b) compute token-Jaccard → lexical score; (c) compute shortest-path distance in FMA graph → tax score (e.g. `tax_sim = decay^distance`); (d) lookup embedding vectors and compute cosine → emb score.  Return the **maximum** of these.  Benchmark and tune the global similarity threshold `t` (e.g. on a small holdout or with human judgments as above).  

In summary, we recommend **(1)** expanding the curated graph with the FMA genre taxonomy, **(2)** improving the co-occurrence embedding by using truncated SVD, **(3)** retaining the existing lexical matching, and **(4)** considering a lightweight audio-based feature if already available.  This hybrid should outperform the current methods: taxonomy handles broad and parent relations, embedding captures usage-based similarity, and lexical catches edge cases.
